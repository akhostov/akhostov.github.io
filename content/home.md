## @hero-lead
I am an observational astrophysicist studying how galaxies form stars, build up heavy elements, and grow within their dark matter halos over the past 12 billion years. I combine large galaxy surveys, deep spectroscopy from JWST and ground-based telescopes, and data-intensive methods, including machine learning, to measure how galaxies evolved.

## @intro-headline
Every galaxy we see today, including the Milky Way, is the product of 13.8 billion years of gas accretion and outflows, star formation, feedback, and chemical enrichment. My research measures how and when that growth happened.

## @intro
Because light travels at a finite speed, more distant galaxies are seen as they were in the past: a galaxy at redshift *z* = 2 is observed as it was about 10.5 billion years ago. Observing galaxies at different distances therefore builds a timeline of how they changed across the Universe's history, and applying the laws of physics to that timeline explains why they changed as they did. I use narrowband surveys, which select thousands of star-forming galaxies in thin slices of cosmic time, together with deep spectroscopy of individual galaxies, to measure how star formation, the abundance of heavy elements, and the masses of the surrounding dark matter halos changed across cosmic history.

Archival data are a recurring part of my work: the large body of past observations can directly support current science. Combining two decades of published and unpublished spectroscopy in the COSMOS field has produced public data sets used across the community and revealed rare objects such as the extreme emission-line galaxy EELG1002.

## @theme-1
Building the COSMOS Spectroscopic Redshift Compilation (487,666 redshifts), using self-organizing maps to identify the galaxy populations that spectroscopic surveys have missed, and developing archival spectroscopic databases that include all published and unpublished data.

## @theme-2
Narrowband surveys of more than 10,000 emission-line galaxies trace the cosmic star-formation history to *z* ≈ 5 and show that galaxies with bursty star formation produced 40–60% of new stars at cosmic noon.

## @theme-3
With JWST, I measured direct, temperature-based oxygen abundances for galaxies 10–12 billion years ago: the largest such sample at cosmic noon, constraining how galaxies became chemically enriched when the Universe was forming stars most actively.

## @theme-4
Clustering measurements of about 12,000 emission-line galaxies reveal a redshift-independent relation between line luminosity and dark matter halo mass, showing that galaxies grow together with their host halos, with evidence that halo quenching suppresses star formation in the most luminous emitters. Archival spectroscopy now makes 3D maps of the cosmic web possible, which I use to study how environment shapes galaxy evolution.

## @highlight-1
Using ultra-deep JWST/NIRSpec spectra from the AURORA survey, I measured direct-method (electron-temperature) oxygen abundances for 34 galaxies at *z* ≈ 1.4–3.5, the largest such sample at cosmic noon. The resulting mass–metallicity relation constrains how galaxies were enriched with heavy elements when the Universe was forming stars most actively.

## @highlight-2
I combined two decades of spectroscopy in the COSMOS field into one public catalog: 487,666 redshifts of 266,284 objects from 138 programs. Self-organizing maps trained on the full photometric catalog reveal which kinds of galaxies still lack spectra, guiding future surveys with Rubin, Euclid, and Roman.

## @highlight-3
In archival Gemini spectra, I identified EELG1002, a galaxy at *z* = 0.83 with a rest-frame [O III]+Hβ equivalent width of about 3,100–3,700 Å, 32–36 times the typical value at its mass and epoch. Its low metallicity and efficient ionizing-photon production make it a close analog of reionization-era galaxies.
